Common AI-Related Risks in Clinical Settings

Understanding where these risks arise helps clinicians recognize why AI systems sometimes fail and how to use them responsibly.

Risk What it means Why it matters clinically Where it arises in the AI lifecycle
Bias and fairness The AI performs differently for certain patients or groups — producing less accurate or less equitable outputs for underrepresented populations. A biased model may systematically underperform for patients of certain ages, races, sexes, or clinical backgrounds — leading to inequitable care recommendations or missed findings. Data collection Model training Evaluation Monitoring
Accuracy and hallucination The AI generates incorrect or fabricated information — producing outputs that sound plausible and confident but are not grounded in fact or the patient record. A hallucinated medication, diagnosis, or clinical detail in an AI-generated summary or note can directly affect patient care if acted on without verification. Model training Evaluation Deployment
Privacy and confidentiality Patient information is exposed, shared, or used in ways that were not authorized — including through inadequate de-identification, third-party data flows, or training data memorization. Patient data used to train or run AI systems may be retained by vendors, used to train future models, or exposed through model outputs — often without patients' knowledge or consent. Data collection Model training Deployment Monitoring
Robustness The AI fails to perform reliably when encountering unusual patients, edge cases, or clinical settings different from those it was trained and validated on. A model that performs well on average may produce incomplete, inaccurate, or dangerously inadequate outputs for complex, atypical, or high-acuity patients — precisely those who need the most reliable information. Model training Evaluation Deployment Monitoring

These risks are interconnected — they can emerge, be amplified, or go undetected across multiple stages of the AI lifecycle. Understanding where they originate helps clinicians recognize why AI sometimes fails and what questions to ask before acting on AI-generated outputs.
