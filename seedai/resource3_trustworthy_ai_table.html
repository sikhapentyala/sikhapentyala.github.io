Landscape of Trustworthy AI

Eight properties that define responsible and trustworthy AI use in clinical practice. The first four relate to understanding how AI can fail; the last four relate to how clinicians evaluate and act on AI outputs.

Property Clinician-facing question What this means in practice
Understand
Bias and fairness		 Does the AI perform differently for certain patients or groups? Check whether the tool was evaluated across different patient demographics — age, race, sex, diagnosis. High overall accuracy can mask poor performance for specific subgroups.
Understand
Accuracy and hallucination		 Can the AI generate incorrect or fabricated information with confidence? AI systems predict plausible text — they do not retrieve verified facts. Any AI-generated clinical detail should be traced back to the source record before being acted on.
Understand
Privacy and confidentiality		 Could patient information be exposed or used inappropriately? Understand where patient data goes when you use an AI tool — vendor servers, data retention policies, and whether data is used to train future models. Consumer AI tools are not covered by institutional data agreements.
Understand
Robustness		 Does the AI continue to perform well with unusual patients, edge cases, or new settings? A model validated at one institution or time period may degrade in your setting. Complex, atypical patients are often where robustness failures appear — and where accurate AI output matters most.
Act responsibly
Transparency		 Are users informed about how the AI was developed, evaluated, and intended to be used? Before using a tool, ask: was it validated on patients like mine? What data was it trained on? What are its known limitations? This information should be available from your institution or the vendor.
Act responsibly
Explainability and faithfulness		 Can users understand why the AI produced a recommendation or summary? Most clinical AI tools cannot fully explain their outputs. When source references are provided, verify that they actually support the AI's conclusions — explanations can appear convincing without faithfully reflecting the model's reasoning.
Act responsibly
Accountability and human oversight		 Who is responsible for reviewing AI outputs and making the final clinical decision? Clinical accountability cannot be delegated to an algorithm. The note you sign, the decision you make — these belong to you regardless of how the AI contributed. Human oversight is non-negotiable.
Act responsibly
Safety		 Could acting on the AI's output lead to patient harm, and how can that risk be reduced? Before acting on any AI output, ask: what is the worst case if this is wrong? Calibrate your verification effort to the stakes — high-stakes outputs require more scrutiny before you act.

The first four properties relate to understanding AI risks — covered in Module 1. The last four relate to using AI responsibly — covered in Module 2. Together they provide a framework for critically evaluating any AI tool you encounter in clinical practice.
